How Watches Work

Movements, escapements, power reserve and case dimensions — the mechanics behind every specification sheet.

Watch marketing is unusually full of words that sound technical and explain nothing. This guide covers what is actually inside the case and which of the numbers on a specification sheet change how the watch feels to own.

Quartz and mechanical

A quartz movement passes a current through a tiny quartz crystal, which vibrates 32,768 times a second. A circuit counts those vibrations and advances the hands. It is accurate to a few seconds a month, costs little to make and needs a battery every two or three years.

A mechanical movement stores energy in a coiled mainspring and releases it in tiny, regular steps through an escapement, regulated by a balance wheel swinging back and forth several times a second. It is accurate to seconds a day rather than seconds a month, contains two hundred or so parts, and can be repaired indefinitely.

Hand-wound and automatic

Both are mechanical. A hand-wound watch is wound through the crown, usually daily. An automatic adds a weighted rotor that swings as your wrist moves and winds the mainspring for you. Automatics are thicker because of the rotor, and they stop after a day or two in a drawer.

Power reserve

This is how long the watch runs from fully wound. Forty hours is traditional, seventy is now common and a few movements manage several days. Anything under about fifty hours means a watch taken off on Friday evening will have stopped by Sunday.

Beat rate

Measured in vibrations per hour: 21,600 is slow and relatively robust, 28,800 is the modern standard and gives a smoother sweep, 36,000 is faster still and demands more frequent servicing. Higher is not automatically better; it balances smoothness against wear.

Case dimensions and fit

Four numbers decide how a watch sits. Diameter is the width across the case. Thickness decides whether it slides under a cuff. Lug-to-lug is the length from tip to tip and must not overhang the flat of your wrist. Lug width sets which straps will fit, and it is usually about 55 per cent of the case diameter.

Our Strap Size Finder takes a wrist measurement and a case width and gives you the proportion, the likely lug width and the strap lengths to look for.

Crystals

Sapphire is extremely hard and resists scratches but can shatter under a sharp knock. Mineral glass is softer and cheaper. Acrylic scratches easily and polishes out just as easily, which is why vintage watches often look better than they should. None of them are unbreakable.

Reading a specification sheet

Look for movement type and reference, power reserve, beat rate, case diameter, thickness, lug-to-lug, lug width, crystal, water resistance rating and whether the crown screws down. If a listing omits thickness and lug-to-lug, it is usually because they are unflattering.

The Care & Service Guide

Winding, setting, magnetism, straps, storage and when to hand it to a watchmaker.

Most watches that stop working were not broken by anything dramatic. They were wound against the stops, set backwards at midnight, left next to a speaker or taken into a hot shower every morning for three years.

Winding and setting

Wind a hand-wound watch at the same time each day, off the wrist, until you feel firm resistance, then stop. Never force it. With an automatic, thirty turns of the crown is plenty to start it from stopped; the rotor does the rest.

Avoid changing the date between about 9 pm and 3 am on any watch with a date complication. The date mechanism is engaged during that window and forcing it can shear a tooth. Move the hands forward past noon first if you are not sure.

Magnetism

A suddenly fast mechanical watch, gaining minutes rather than seconds, is usually magnetised. Speaker magnets, laptop closures, handbag clasps and induction hobs all do it. Demagnetising takes a watchmaker about two minutes and is often free. It is the single most common cause of alarming timekeeping.

Water, in practice

Printed ratings come from static pressure tests on new watches. Gaskets harden with age, heat accelerates it and soap reduces surface tension so water finds its way in more easily. The practical rules: screw the crown down, keep the watch out of hot showers, rinse it in fresh water after the sea, and have the seals pressure-tested every couple of years.

The Water Resistance Decoder translates each rating into what it permits, and adjusts for how long ago the seals were checked.

Changing straps without damage

Use a proper spring bar tool, not a screwdriver, and tape the case so a slip does not score the lugs. Work from the back. On a quick-release strap, slide the small lever and the bar retracts with no tool at all. Replace spring bars every few strap changes; they are inexpensive and they do fail.

Storage

Keep watches away from heat, direct sun and anything magnetic. A soft pouch or a lined box is enough. Winders look appealing but are not necessary and keep a movement running, and wearing, for no reason. If a watch will sit for months, let it stop; mainsprings do not suffer from being at rest.

When a service is due

Every five to seven years for a mechanical watch in regular use, or sooner if the power reserve drops, the rate drifts badly or the crown feels rough. A service means dismantling the movement, cleaning every part, replacing worn components and gaskets, re-oiling, reassembling and regulating. It takes hours of skilled work, which is why it is not cheap and why a good watchmaker is worth keeping.

Quartz watches need less, but have the gaskets replaced whenever the back is opened for a battery, and replace a dead battery promptly: a leaking cell destroys a movement in months.

A simple routine

Wipe the case and bracelet with a soft cloth weekly. Rinse with fresh water after the sea. Check the rate for a week once a year using the Accuracy Checker. Pressure test the seals every two years. Service on schedule. Watches looked after this way outlive their owners, which is rather the point of them.
